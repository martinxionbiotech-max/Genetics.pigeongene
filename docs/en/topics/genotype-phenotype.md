# Genotype and Phenotype in Pigeons

The genotype is the combination of genes; the phenotype is the observed trait. A genetic test reads the genotype while the fancier sees the phenotype — understanding the relationship between the two is the precondition for using a test report correctly.

## Plain-language reading (an analogy)

The genotype is like a **script** and the phenotype like a **performance**. With the same script (genotype), different stages, actors and rehearsal conditions (environment, training, nutrition) can produce a very different performance (phenotype).

## How genotype and phenotype correspond

- **Single-gene traits**: one pair of alleles is enough to determine the trait (some plumage colour traits, for example), so the correspondence is direct;
- **Polygenic traits**: race results, body type and similar traits are governed by the additive effects of many genes, so a single-locus genotype supplies only part of the information;
- **Phenotypic plasticity**: the same genotype gives different phenotypes in different environments — one genetic explanation for the "form fluctuations" seen in racing pigeons.

## Genotype + environment = phenotype

This is the basic equation of quantitative genetics. For racing pigeons:

- The genotype sets the **ceiling of potential**;
- The environment (training, nutrition, health, stress) determines **how far that potential is realised**;
- Genotype-by-environment interaction makes individual performance more complex again.

## What a test report actually reads

A genetic test report gives the **genotype at marker loci** — the combination of alleles an individual carries at particular SNP/microsatellite loci. It answers the question "what type is this pigeon at a given locus", not "how fast can this pigeon fly".

## From genotype to decisions

- The genotype is used as a **pairing reference**: understanding the parents' genetic background and planning combinations;
- The phenotype is used for **performance verification**: the offspring's race results are the final test of how well a pairing worked;
- The two update each other: good bloodline + good results + good genotype = more complete breeding information.

## Common misconceptions

- "A good genotype means a good phenotype" — this ignores the environment and polygenic interaction;
- "A good phenotype means a good genotype" — a pigeon in form because of its environment may have an ordinary genetic basis;
- "One test settles it for life" — a test is a snapshot; breeding is a long race.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do pigeons with the same genotype have different phenotypes?**

Environmental differences, gene-expression regulation and random developmental variation all produce divergence in phenotype.

**Is a pigeon with a good phenotype worth breeding from?**

Race results are an important reference, but we suggest assessing bloodline, inbreeding background and genetic testing together.

**Can a test report give breeding recommendations directly?**

The report provides genotype information; breeding decisions still need to combine race results, bloodline and the actual conditions of the loft.
