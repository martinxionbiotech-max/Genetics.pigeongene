# Patterns of Inheritance in Pigeons

Patterns of inheritance are the basis for understanding "why good pigeons produce good pigeons". This page explains Mendel's laws and the basic concepts of quantitative inheritance in plain language, along with how they map onto racing pigeon breeding.

## Plain-language reading (an analogy)

Inheritance is like **shuffling and dealing cards**: each parent contributes half the "cards" (gametes), and the offspring receives a freshly shuffled combination. The cards are still the same deck, but the combinations vary endlessly — which is why two brothers from the same nest can end up with wildly different results.

## Mendel's laws: the rules for single-gene traits

From his pea-crossing experiments, Mendel summarised two basic laws:

- **Law of segregation**: every individual carries a pair of alleles; they separate at random when gametes form, and the offspring receives one allele from each parent;
- **Law of independent assortment**: genes on different chromosomes are distributed independently when gametes form.

Dominance and recessiveness determine the phenotype of a heterozygous individual: a dominant allele is expressed even in the heterozygous state, while a recessive allele is expressed only when homozygous.

## Quantitative traits: the genetic nature of race results

Flying ability and race results are **quantitative traits** — decided by the additive effects of many genes together with the environment, with a continuous phenotypic distribution rather than a "present/absent" dichotomy. This means:

- A single gene contributes only a limited share to race results;
- Offspring results are distributed around the parental mean, with regression towards the mean;
- Breeding improvement is a gradual, cumulative process.

## Heritability: the "gold content" of a trait's inheritance

Heritability measures the proportion of phenotypic variation attributable to genetic factors, and runs from 0 to 1. For traits with high heritability, phenotypic selection works well; for traits with low heritability, outcomes depend more on environment and management. Race performance is a composite trait of medium heritability — a good bloodline provides the foundation, while training and nutrition determine how much of the ceiling is reached.

## Relationship to genetic testing

Genetic testing looks at the **genotype at particular marker loci**, which is locus-level information, whereas race results are an individual-level composite outcome. The right way to combine them is: use test results to understand genetic background, use race results to verify breeding outcomes — each a reference for the other, neither able to replace the other.

## Common misconceptions

- "The offspring of a champion are bound to be good" — inheritance is probabilistic, not copy-and-paste;
- "Testing one gene settles the winner" — quantitative traits need many genes and many generations of accumulation;
- "A pedigree equals a results sheet" — a pedigree records lineage; results are what tests the phenotype.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a dominant gene always "better"?**

No. Dominance and recessiveness describe only the rules of expression; they do not judge merit. Whether something is "good" depends on the relationship between the trait and the environment.

**Why do pigeons from the same nest differ so much?**

Random combination of gametes leaves nestmates with different genotypes, and environmental differences on top of that make phenotypes diverge naturally.

**Can heritability be calculated precisely?**

It takes large amounts of pedigree and phenotype records and statistical analysis. An ordinary loft can hardly estimate it precisely, but can draw on long experience in breeding practice.
